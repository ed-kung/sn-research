\section{Estimating $\ln v(q)$} \label{sec_embeddings}

\subsection{Description of the procedure}

Let $p$ index a post and let $Z_p$ be the amount of tips it receives in the first 48 hours. As described in equation \eqref{eq_v4v_lnzp}, we model $Z_p$ as:
\begin{align}
\ln Z_p = \mu_{i(p)j(p)} + \underbrace{\delta_{w(p)} + g(X_p)}_{\ln v(q_p)} + \varepsilon_p \tag{\ref{eq_v4v_lnzp}}
\end{align}
and we estimate each post's quality adjusted zap signal, $\hat{r}_p$ as:
\begin{align}
\hat{r}_p &= \ln Z_p - \widehat{\ln v(q)} \nonumber \\
& = \ln Z_p - \hat{\delta}_{w(p)}-\hat{g}(X_p) \tag{\ref{eq_v4v_rhat}}
\end{align}
This section describes our procedure for estimating equation \eqref{eq_v4v_lnzp} and thereby recovering estimates for $\hat{\delta}_{w(p)}$ and $\hat{g}(X_p)$. Since it is a mixed model with both linear fixed effects and a non-parametric function of $X_p$, we estimate it iteratively. Let $i$ now index a turn in the iterative procedure instead of a user. The procedure starts at turn $i=0$ with $\hat{g}^{(0)}(X_p)=0$. At each turn $i+1$, the procedure first uses OLS to estimate the fixed effects:
\begin{align}
\hat{\mu}^{(i+1)}_{i(p)j(p)},~ \hat{\delta}^{(i+1)}_{w(p)} = \text{OLS Regression of } \left(\ln Z_p - \hat{g}^{(i)}(X_p) \right) \text{ on } \mu_{i(p)j(p)},\delta_{w(p)}
\end{align}
and then uses XGBoost to estimate $\hat{g}^{(i+1)}$ using the new fixed effects:
\begin{align}
\hat{g}^{(i+1)}(X_p) = \text{XGBoost Regression of } \left( \ln Z_p - \hat{\mu}^{(i+1)}_{i(p)j(p)} - \hat{\delta}^{(i+1)}_{w(p)} \right) \text{ on } X_p \label{eq_apdx_xgboost}
\end{align}

The procedure can be run a fixed number of times or iterate until convergence. At the end of the procedure, we estimate the fixed effects a final time and use it to compute an estimate for each post's quality adjusted zap signal, as per equation \eqref{eq_v4v_rhat}.


\subsection{Calculation of embeddings}

Text embeddings were computed for each post by inputting the post text into \mbox{OpenAI's} \texttt{text-embedding-3-small} embedding model. The embedding model computes a 1,536 dimensional embedding vector for each post that encodes the meaning of the text in such a way that two posts with similar text will have a small distance between their embedding vectors. The raw embedding dimensions are learned from a large general purpose training corpus and are not tailored to the structure of our dataset. We therefore compute the principal components of the raw embedding vectors using our corpus. The principal components are orthogonal linear combinations of the original 1,536 embedding dimensions that explain the greatest variation across posts in our data. Figure \ref{fig_text_scree_plot} shows the scree plot of the principal components analysis, for the first \gn{NumPCAComponentsShow} components. To keep our regression in \eqref{eq_apdx_xgboost} parsimonious, we decided to use only the first \gn{TextPCAK} components in the analysis. These components explain  \gn{TextPCAPctExplained}\% of the total variation,  but our results in Section \ref{sec_v4v} are robust to the specific choice of how many components to use. 

\subsection{Estimation results}

Table \ref{tbl_xgboost} reports the root mean squared errror and $R^2$ statistics for the estimation procedure for three iterations. It also compares the metrics to an OLS benchmark. The non-parametric fixed effects model outperforms the OLS model, and the predictive accuracy converges quickly after a few iterations. 

To get a sense of which factors are more important for explaining $\ln Z_p$, we present Table \ref{tbl_xgboost_factors}. On the left hand side of the table, we show how much each of $\hat{\mu}_{i(p)j(p)}$, $\delta_{w(p)}$, and $\hat{g}(X_p)$ each contribute to the variance of $\widehat{\ln Z_p}$.\footnote{Note that if $Y = a + b +c$ then the percent of $Y$'s variance explained by $a$ is $\text{Cov}(a,Y)/\text{Var}(Y)$.} On the right hand side of the table, we show the ``permutation importance'' of each of the variables in $X_p$. The permutation importance measures how much the RMSE would increase if the model was re-estimated using shuffled values for that variable. In computing the permutation importance, we only re-estimate $\hat{g}(X_p)$ at the last iterative step, holding the fixed effect estimates $\hat{\mu}_{i(p)j(p)}$ and $\delta_{w(p)}$ fixed.

The left half Table \ref{tbl_xgboost_factors} shows that user-territory fixed effects explain most of the variance in the predictor, about \gn{usfePctExplained}\%. Week fixed effects explain \gn{weekfePctExplained}\% of the variance, and $\hat{g}(X_p)$ explains the remaining \gn{xgbPctExplained}\%. Note that the combined model explains only about 50\% of the variance in log zaps, so we can halve the previous numbers to estimate how much each components contributes to the variance of log zaps. The relatively small explanatory power of $\hat{g}(X_p)$ suggests that post quality is hard to measure directly from objective attributes like word count, image count, and coarse semantic measures. The relatively high explanatory power of user-territory fixed effects suggests that user-territory match quality is an important factor and that users, to the extent that they are uncertain about their territory-specific match qualities, are incentivized to learn about them.

The right half of Table \ref{tbl_xgboost_factors} shows the permutation importance of the variables in $X_p$. The number of words in the post is the most important factor, followed by the first text embedding dimension, followed by whether or not it is a link post. Instead of trying to interpret the meaning of each embedding dimension, we inspect the posts with highest and lowest values of $\hat{g}(X_p)$ in an attempt to characterize the objective measurements that lead to greater or lesser zaps. We found that the posts in the 95th or higher percentiles of $\hat{g}(X_p)$ were mostly long-form ideological or technical posts about Bitcoin or Lightning, posts about Stacker News itself, or posts about monetary economics. Since $X_p$ does not contain territory information, this means that the semantic vectors are able to identify such posts. By contrast, posts in the 5th or lower percentiles of $\hat{g}(X_p)$ were more diverse. They include repetitive daily posts that some users make that others tune out, short posts with barely any text, and posts that look like they might have been generated with AI.
