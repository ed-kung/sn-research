\section{Tip-based learning}\label{sec_v4v}

We now consider our second hypothesis: that users learn from and respond to tip feedback by shifting their posting behavior towards better rewarded post types. 

\subsection{Model of tip-based learning}

We start by developing a model of learning from tip feedback. Because learning involves many unobservables, like users' priors, information sets, and sources of uncertainty, we aim to keep the model parsimonious. The purpose of the model is to illustrate how assumptions about the decision structure lead to testable predictions, rather than to deeply micro-found the assumptions.\footnote{In the appendix, we develop a more microfounded model and discuss how they map onto our assumptions here.}

We assume that users are learning about their expected rewards in territories. Let $i$ index a user and let $j$ index a territory. The expected present value of the Poisson stream of zaps that user $i$ receives in territory $j$ is:
\begin{align}
V(q; \Theta_{ij}) = \Theta_{ij} v(q)  \label{eq_v4v_V}
\end{align}
where $v(q)$ is known, continuous, and strictly increasing in $q$. $\Theta_{ij}$ is an unknown, user-territory specific match parameter which reflects how well user $i$'s posts are suited for territory $j$, holding objective post quality fixed.

Let $\mathcal{I}_{it}$ denote user $i$'s information at time $t$, which includes previously observed tip rewards. Define $A_{ijt}$ and $\alpha_{ijt}$ as the expected value and the log expected value of $\Theta_{ij}$ conditional on the user's information set:
\begin{align}
A_{ijt} = E\left[ \Theta_{ij} \mid \mathcal{I}_{it} \right], ~ ~ \alpha_{ijt} = \ln A_{ijt}
\end{align}

Post ideas for territory $j$ arrive for user $i$ at rate $\gamma_{ij}$, and their quality and intrinsic motivation are distributed according to $f^{ij}(q,\epsilon) = f^{ij}_{\epsilon|q}(\epsilon|q)f^{ij}_q(q)$. As in Section \ref{sec_pay_to_post_model}, we assume that within each territory, $f^{ij}(q,\epsilon)$ satisfies log concavity and the monotone likelihood ratio property.

If a post idea for territory $j$ arrives for user $i$ at time $t$ with quality and intrinsic motivation $(q,\epsilon)$, the posting rule is post if:
\begin{align}
\alpha_{ijt} + \ln v(q) + \epsilon > \kappa_{jt}
\end{align}
where $\kappa_{jt} = \ln c_{jt}$ is the log posting cost in territory $j$ at time $t$.  The probability (over $q, \epsilon$) that the idea is posted is given by:
\begin{align}
P_{ijt} = \int S^{ij}_{\epsilon|q} \left( \kappa_{jt} - \alpha_{ijt} - \ln v(q) \mid q \right) f^{ij}_q(q) dq
\end{align}
which is decreasing in $\kappa_{jt}$ and increasing in $\alpha_{ijt}$. Thus, higher posting costs deter posting, but a greater expectation of tip rewards incentivizes posting. The instantaneous rate of user $i$ posting in territory $j$ at time $t$ is $\gamma_{ij} P_{ijt}$.

\subsection{Empirical specification}

To bring the model to the data, we model belief updating at a weekly frequency. This need not imply that users formally update their beliefs once a week, but it captures the idea that behavioral responses to tip feedback are gradual and that high-frequency variation in individual posting decisions is largely idiosyncratic. The temporal aggregation also permits territory-by-week fixed effects that absorb common changes in territory-level attention and posting incentives, allowing learning to be identified from variation in individual post histories. 

We also model users as updating their beliefs using tips that a post receives after 48 hours, $Z$. This keeps the model parsimonious, because $\ln Z$ is a noisy signal of $\theta_{ij} + \ln v(q)$, and it allows us to abstract away from details about the timing of when posts receive their zaps.\footnote{To see this, we note that $E[Z] \propto V(q) = \Theta_{ij}v(q)$, according to equations \eqref{eq_V} and \eqref{eq_v4v_V}. We can therefore write $Z = \Theta_{ij} v(q) e^{\eta}$, or $\ln Z = \theta_{ij} + \ln v(q) + \eta$, where $\eta$ is an i.i.d. residual arising from the idiosyncratic realizations of the Poisson zaps process.} We therefore assume that users update their beliefs according to their history of ``quality-adjusted zap signals'': $r = \ln Z - \ln v(q)$.

Let $\mathcal{I}_{ijw}$ be user $i$'s information set for territory $j$ at the start of week $w$. Let $p$ index a post, sorted by timestamp, and let $i(p)$ be the user who made the post, $j(p)$ be the territory of the post, and $\tau(p)$ be the timestamp of the post. Let $\tau(w)$ be the timestamp of the start of a week. The information set available to user $i$ for territory $j$ at the start of week $w$ is:
\begin{align}
\mathcal{I}_{ijw} &= \left\{ (Z_p, q_p):p\in \mathcal{P}_{ijw} \right\}\\
\mathcal{P}_{ijw} &= \left\{ p: \tau(p) < \tau(w) - \text{48 hours}, ~ i(p)=i, ~j(p)=j \right\}
\end{align}
In words, the user is able to observe signals for all their posts made in the territory 48 hours before the start of the week.

We postulate the following reduced form specification for $\alpha_{ijt}$, the log of the user's posterior mean over $\Theta_{ij}$ at the start of week $w$:
\begin{align}
\alpha_{ijw} &= b^0_{ij} + \beta \sum_{p \in \mathcal{P}_{iw}} K_{ijwp} \left[ \ln Z_p - \ln v(q_p) \right] \nonumber \\
& = b^0_{ij} + \beta \sum_{p \in \mathcal{P}_{iw}} K_{ijwp} r_{p} 
\end{align}
In words, the log of the user's posterior mean over $\Theta_{ij}$ is a weighted sum of previously observed quality-adjusted zap signals, $r_p : p\in \mathcal{P}_{ijw}$. This specification nests standard models of learning, such as conjugate-normal Bayesian updating, but it is flexible enough to allow for effects like attention decay or drift. For example, suppose we assume that users pay more attention to recent signals. Then, we could specify the kernel weights as:
\begin{align}
K_{ijwp} \propto e^{-(\tau(w) - \tau(p))/\sigma} 
\end{align}
However, we need not specify the weights in this manner, and in practice we can try different assumptions about the information environment.

Now, let $E \left[ N_{ijw} \mid \mathcal{I}_{ijw} \right]$ be the expected number of posts user $i$ will make in territory $j$ in week $t$, conditional in the information set $\mathcal{I}_{ijw}$. Under our beliefs model, the exact form is equal to:
\begin{align}
E \left[ N_{ijw} \mid \mathcal{I}_{ijw} \right] = \gamma_{ij} \int_{\tau(w)}^{\tau(w+1)} \int S_{\epsilon|q}^{ij} \left( \kappa_{jt} - \alpha_{ijw} - \ln v(q) \mid q \right) f_q^{ij}(q) dq dt \label{eq_v4v_EN}
\end{align}
This expression shows that the number of posts in week $w$ is expected to depend on:
\begin{itemize}
\item User-by-territory effects; namely, a user-territory-specific arrival rate of post ideas, $\gamma_{ij}$, and user-territory-specific distribution of post quality and intrinsic motivation, $f^{ij}(q,\epsilon)$. This captures permanent differences in users' preferences and ability to post in different territories.
\item Territory-by-time effects; namely, the posting cost of the territory at any time $t$, $\kappa_{jt}$. In the reduced form, territory-by-time effects could also capture topic-specific trends, such as those driven by news cycles, that are not user-specific.
\item A weighted sum of previously observed quality-adjusted zap signals, $\alpha_{ijw}$. Higher past signals will lead to a higher expected number of posts in the week.
\end{itemize}
To turn this into a reduced form estimating equation, we model the number of posts as a Poisson distribution:
\begin{align}
N_{ijw} & \sim \text{Poisson}(\mu_{ijw}) \nonumber \\
\ln \mu_{ijw} &= \beta X_{ijw} + \xi_{ij} + \zeta_{jw} + \nu_{iw} + \varepsilon_{ijw} \nonumber \\
X_{ijw} &= \sum_{p \in \mathcal{P}_{ijw}} K_{ijwp} r_{p}    \label{eq_v4v_estimator}
\end{align}
User-by-territory fixed effects, $\xi_{ij}$, capture user-specific preferences and tendencies to post in specific territories. Territory-by-week fixed effects, $\zeta_{jw}$, capture territory-specific time trends as well as variation in posting costs. We also add user-by-week fixed effects $\nu_{iw}$ to capture changing non-territory-specific user motivation over time. The model is estimated using Poisson pseudo-maximum likelihood, as implemented in R's \texttt{fixest::fepois} package.

A few comments on identification. First, identification of learning comes from user-level variation in territory-specific signals. If all users had access to the same signals and formed the same expectations about territory rewards, then this would be fully absorbed into $\zeta_{jw}$. Second, the main threat to identification comes from user-by-territory specific time trends. For example, if users have time-varying territory-specific ability, then past observed signals may reflect growing ability, which is also correlated with subsequent posting. For the purposes of this paper, we do not really care whether the learning is about a fixed or temporal user-territory match; in either case the result is that tip feedback affects user posting decisions. So if users are learning from tip feedback about temporal ability, then we do not consider that a threat to identification. A correlation between past signals and subsequent posting is only spurious if the subsequent posting would have happened regardless of what the prior tip feedback was, in a way that the correlation only exists as coincidence. An example of this would be a news cycle that generates high rewards for specific topics, but that only some users are heavily interested in posting about. To the extent that the effect of the news cycle is not captured in $\ln(q)$, it would show up in both higher quality-adjusted zap signals and increased number of posts, but the increased number of posts would have happened regardless of the higher signals. Without purely exogenous variation in signals, we cannot rule out such threats to identification. Our results should therefore be interpreted as evidence consistent with learning rather than a tightly identified causal estimate of learning.

\subsection{Results}

We now consider estimation of equation \eqref{eq_v4v_estimator}. We estimate the equation with three different sets of weights:
\begin{align}
X_{ijw}^U &= \sum_{p \in \mathcal{P}_{ijw}} K_{ijwp}^U r_p, ~ ~ ~ K_{ijwp}^U \propto 1 \\
X_{ijw}^R &= \sum_{p \in \mathcal{P}_{ijw}} K_{ijwp}^R r_p, ~ ~ ~ K_{ijwp}^R \propto e^{-(\tau(w) - \tau(p))/\sigma}  \\
X_{ijw}^E &= \sum_{p \in \mathcal{P}_{ijw}} K_{ijwp}^E r_p, ~ ~ ~ K_{ijwp}^E \propto \frac{1}{\sqrt{n_{ij}(p)}} 
\end{align}
$X_{ijw}^U$ is the weighted average of all user $i$'s past signals in territory $j$, with uniform weights. $X_{ijw}^R$ puts more weight on recent signals, with an exponential time decay with half-life of 8 weeks. Users may weigh recent signals more if there is temporal drift in the learning target or if their memory decays over time. $X_{ijw}^E$ puts more weight on early signals. $n_{ij}(p)$ is the rank order of post $p$ in user $i$'s territory $j$ post history; i.e. $n_{ij}(p)=1$ if $p$ is the first post, $n_{ij}(p)=2$ if it's the second post, and so on. Earlier signals may carry more weight if attention is costly, leading to an entrenchment of beliefs after a few early signals, or if users learn the stable component of the match early and later signals mostly reflect transitory shocks.

To calculate $r_p$, we first estimate $\ln v(q)$ as a non-parametric function of objective post characteristics $X_p$. These characteristics include the word count, whether the post is a link post, the number of links and images in the post, and a 10-dimensional semantic embedding vector of the post text. The estimator minimizes the mean squared error in the equation:
\begin{align}
\ln Z_p = \mu_{i(p)j(p)} + \underbrace{\delta_{w(p)} + g(X_p)}_{\ln v(q_p)} + \varepsilon_p
\end{align}
and we construct:
\begin{align}
\hat{r}_p = \ln Z_p - \hat{\delta}_{w(p)}-\hat{g}(X_p)
\end{align}
User-territory fixed effects $\mu_{i(p)j(p)}$ are included in the regression but not included in the construction of $\hat{r}_p$ because that is the parameter users are learning about. Week fixed effects $\delta_{w(p)}$ are included in the regression to capture global time trends that are assumed to be known to users. We describe the non-parametric estimation of $\hat{g}(X_p)$ in more detail in the appendix.

The results of regression \eqref{eq_v4v_estimator} using $X_{ijw}^U$, $X_{ijw}^R$, $X_{ijw}^E$ are reported in columns 1-3 of Table \ref{tbl_learning_reg}. All three coefficients are positive and statistically significant, which means prior tip signals are positively correlated current propensity to post. To check whether recent or early signals matter more, we also estimate a model in which all three of $X_{ijw}^U$, $X_{ijw}^R$, and $X_{ijw}^E$ are included in the regression. Since each is a weighted average of prior signals, the sum is a linear combination of the kernel weights, and we let the data tell us which weighting scheme better explains the data. The results from this combined regression are reported in column 4 of Table \ref{tbl_learning_reg}. Interestingly, the results show that the data is best fit by placing more weight on the uniform kernel than either the recency-based or the early-signals kernel. Although not statistically significant at the 10\% level, the coefficients on $X_{ijw}^R$ and $X_{ijw}^E$ in column 4 are negative, suggesting (if anything) that users pay more attention medium-term signals than either recent signals or early signals. In column 5 of Table \ref{tbl_learning_reg}, we also test whether the effect of past signals is moderated by the user's experience level. We measure experience as log one plus the number of posts the user has made in the territory prior to week $w$. We find that experienced users are somewhat less responsive to past feedback than inexperienced users. This suggests that experienced users may have already developed sharp beliefs or habits of posting behavior, making them less responsive overall to new signals.

Using column 1 as our preferred specification (since estimates from column 4 are noisier), we estimate that a 10 percent increase in prior quality-adjusted zap signals would increase posting frequency by \gn{LearningEffect10pct} percent. This is quite a modest increase, but we do note that the standard deviation of $X_{ijw}^U$ is quite large at \gn{K1sd}. A one standard deviation increase in $X_{ijw}^U$ is estimated by the model to increase post frequency by \gn{LearningEffectK1sd} percent, a much more significant number.

~

\noindent \emph{Identification.}  The lack of statistical significance and the small coefficient magnitude of the recency-weighted kernel appears to rule out news-cycle and other contemporaneous effects as alternative explanations for why prior signals are correlated with current posting. If the correlation was driven by news cycles or temporal trends, we would expect recent signals to be more correlated with current posting than past signals. Instead, we find that the correlation between prior signals and current posting is best explained by a uniform average of prior signals. The main threat to identification would be an unobserved user-territory specific condition which is both (i) varying over time and (ii) causes past signals to be correlated with current posting in such a way that doesn't give more weight to either recent or early signals. Although we cannot rule out some possible confounder that satisfies these conditions, the range of alternative explanations that could match these patterns seems narrow.


\subsection{Discussion}

In an online message board, there are many potential unknown parameters that users are learning about, and there are many potential sources of information to learn from. We have demonstrated evidence which is consistent with one particular  unknown parameter and one particular source of information: users learning about their user-territory specific match quality using quality-adjusted tip signals from their own posts. This was demonstrated by showing that users who received higher than expected feedback from their posts in a territory are more likely to subsequently post in that territory. The correlation cannot be driven by a time-invariant user-specific match quality, nor can it be driven by a contemporaneous confounder which drives both recent feedback and current posting. The most plausible explanation is that users learn continually from tip feedback and adjust their posting behavior as a result.

Of course, there are many other potential learning-driven behavioral dynamics that we did not investigate. For example, we did not investigate social learning (users learning from other users' posts), nor did we investigate learning about post characteristics (users learning about what attributes of a post are better rewarded). We think these and other dynamics are worth investigating, but we leave it to future research to do so.
